Dear Guest Editors,

Please consider the enclosed manuscript, "Revealed Aspiration Levels: Inverse Interval Goal Programming for Recovering the Targets Behind Public Decisions", for the Annals of Operations Research special issue "Multiple Objective Programming and Goal Programming: Artificial Intelligence for Decision Making in Economic and Social Sciences". The manuscript is original, has not been published, and is not under consideration elsewhere.

**Contribution to multiple objective programming and goal programming.** Goal programming normally starts from aspiration levels and priorities supplied by a decision maker. The manuscript studies the inverse problem: recovering aspiration levels, tolerance bands and step sizes of an interval goal program from a sequence of observed decisions. We prove that optimal decisions lie at breakpoints, that only effective weights are identifiable, that aspiration levels are revealed exactly when a decision stops at a band edge, and that the type of adjustment cost determines what can be learned: with a linear cost the weights are not point-identified, while with a quadratic cost (gradual adjustment) they are, under a rank condition when the other parameters are known. A change of variables makes all optimality conditions linear in the unknowns. For one goal we give the optimal policy in closed form, an exact polynomial-time estimator and a consistency result; for several goals, a mixed-integer formulation that certifies small instances (up to 20 periods in our experiments) and a multistart heuristic without guarantees for the sizes of the applications.

**Contribution to artificial intelligence for decision making.** The method learns a goal program from data. We give a scalable learning algorithm that combines parallel multistart local search on the decision loss with exact linear-programming and large-neighbourhood polishing, document its accuracy on synthetic data with known parameters, and quantify uncertainty by block bootstrap. Placebo experiments show when the fit diagnostics alone would mislead. Two tests, one of state dependence and one against partial adjustment, with size checked by simulation and combined under multiplicity adjustment, guard against reading targets into behaviour that has none; we state that a rejection of partial adjustment shows nonlinear state dependence, not goal programming specifically.

**Contribution to economic and social sciences.** Public actors announce targets; their decisions reveal what they pursue. We check the method on central banks, whose targets are public: the implied targets are within about 0.6 percentage points of the stated targets on average, as a constant 2.5% is, and the one bank where they are much closer than average inflation (Turkey) is a boundary fit; we say so. We then estimate revealed aid targets for 23 donor countries. Under a combined test at a 10% false discovery rate, one to three donors show nonlinear state dependence inconsistent with partial adjustment; Sweden and Norway reveal targets of 0.90 and 0.98% of GNI, close to their national 1% goals, although whether the goals lie inside the uncertainty ranges depends on how the level is measured, and most EU donors reveal targets well below their 0.7% commitment. For NATO allies the revealed lower edge of spending rose from about 2% to 3.2% of GDP after 2022; the bootstrap separates the two periods but the profile loss does not, and no test distinguishes defence decisions from simpler behaviour, so we present this result as descriptive. We report where the method is less reliable (outcomes already at target; a weakly determined pre-2022 defence edge; corporate leverage with a single goal).

All data are public (BIS, OECD, NATO, SEC EDGAR), and the code that reproduces every table is available at https://github.com/vinhqdang/Multiple-Objective-Programming-and-Goal-Programming.
