# Domain note: foreign aid allocation (written 2026-11-22)

Scope: determinants of aid allocation and donor motives, revealed donor preferences, performance-based allocation (World Bank IDA), optimisation treatments, and the one directly related inverse-optimisation paper. Data and ground-truth targets are in `data/aid/` (see `data/aid/README.md`, `data/aid/targets.csv`). Every claim below comes from a text I opened; "read scope" says how much. The search was not systematic.

## Karami-Fard (2026), the directly related paper: not obtained in full
- Ali Karami-Fard (University of Massachusetts Dartmouth per OpenAlex), "Need, Alignment, or Commerce? Recovering the Revealed Priorities of Foreign Aid Donors through Inverse Optimization", SSRN, DOI 10.2139/ssrn.7302189, posted 2026. Crossref and OpenAlex list it as an SSRN preprint (OpenAlex: green OA, no PDF link).
- Full text: not accessible. `doi.org` redirects to papers.ssrn.com, which returns HTTP 403; DataCite has no record; web searches found no author page, slides, conference program or mirror. Recorded as unavailable; try a browser download from SSRN.
- Read scope: the Crossref abstract only. What it says: each donor-year is a constrained allocation problem over three objectives (recipient need, diplomatic alignment, commerce); a KL-divergence-based inverse-optimisation estimator recovers one weight per objective per donor-year, with a goodness-of-fit diagnostic and bootstrap confidence intervals; 24 donors, 142 recipients, 2010-2022. Reported results: need plus alignment fits almost nothing (mean rho about 0.013); adding commerce raises mean fit to about 0.27; France, Japan, Germany at the commerce pole, Belgium, Netherlands and Nordics at the need pole; alignment matters mainly for Gulf donors; post-2018 commercial drift in the UK; results insensitive to a diminishing-returns parameter alpha.
- Relevance and gap relative to our project (from the abstract only; check the full text): objectives are fixed to three proxies and only weights are recovered; no aspiration levels (targets) and no goal-programming deviation structure; no use of publicly stated targets (0.7%, LDC share) for validation. Our design differs on exactly these points. Their data window (2010-2022) and recipient count (142) are close to what `data/aid/` supports, so results are comparable.

## Papers read (full text unless stated)

### 1. Alesina and Dollar, "Who Gives Foreign Aid to Whom and Why?"
- NBER Working Paper 6612, June 1998, DOI 10.3386/w6612 (published in Journal of Economic Growth 5(1), March 2000, DOI 10.1023/A:1009874203400 per Crossref). Read: full text of the NBER working paper (scanned PDF read via OCR, so digits may carry OCR errors; no numbers from tables are used here).
- WHY: is aid driven by recipient need and policy or by political and strategic motives? HOW: DAC bilateral flows converted to constant 1985 dollars, averaged over five-year periods 1970-74 to 1990-94; the log of aid is regressed on income per capita (linear and quadratic), population, trade openness, democracy and civil liberties, colonial status, UN voting alignment with the donor, and dummies for Egypt and Israel; donor-by-donor regressions. WHAT: colonial past and political alliances are the main determinants; at the margin democratisation raises aid (roughly a 50 percent increase after a transition; the digits come from OCR text); Nordic donors respond more to income and institutions, France to former colonies, the US to the Middle East; FDI responds to economic policy rather than politics.
- Relevance: the baseline "need versus strategy" specification, and the source of the covariate set (UN votes, colony) for alignment and commerce goals. Reduced-form weights in a log-linear model, not a trade-off model.

### 2. Hoeffler and Outram, "Need, Merit or Self-Interest: What Determines the Allocation of Aid?"
- CSAE Working Paper WPS/2008-19, Oxford, July 2008 (ORA copy); published in Review of Development Economics 15(2), 237-250, 2011, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9361.2011.00605.x. Read: working-paper text through data and methods and the abstract; the 2011 journal version was not accessed, and the working paper may differ.
- WHY: donors state different aims (they quote German and US mission statements), yet allocations may not differ. HOW: 22 DAC donors, 186 recipients, 1980-2004, aid commitments in constant 2003 dollars, 92,400 potential dyad-years of which 45,289 are positive; donor and recipient effects, fixed effects and selection (Tobit and Heckman discussed). WHAT: roughly half of predicted aid is donor-specific effects; of the remainder, need accounts for 36 percent, self-interest about 16 percent, merit about 2 percent.
- Relevance: a variance-decomposition ground truth for "need versus self-interest" at the donor level, and an explicit warning that donor fixed effects dominate, which for inverse GP means the donor-year weights are not separable from donor-specific constants such as geography and history. Also a caution that they use commitments, whereas `data/aid/` holds disbursements.

### 3. Berthelemy, "Bilateral Donors' Interest vs. Recipients' Development Motives in Aid Allocation: Do All Donors Behave the Same?"
- Working paper 2005.01 (TEAM, Universite Paris 1), HAL halshs-00193273, CC BY 4.0; published Review of Development Economics 10(2), 179-194, 2006, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9361.2006.00311.x. Read: abstract, introduction and result tables (scanned file read via OCR); I did not read every section.
- WHY: test whether donors differ in the weight on self-interest versus altruism. HOW: three-dimensional panel (donor, recipient, year) with Heckman selection, OLS, random effects and fixed effects specifications, with formal tests of equality of parameters across donors. WHAT (from the abstract): large heterogeneity across donors; the paper offers a way to compare the degree of altruism of donors.
- Relevance: the closest econometric analogue of "recover each donor's weights"; it tests donor differences formally, which an inverse GP would need to match with set-valued or interval estimates. Selection (donors give to only some recipients) is the key practical problem.

### 4. Dollar and Levin, "The Increasing Selectivity of Foreign Aid, 1984-2002"
- World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 3299, 2004, DOI 10.1596/1813-9450-3299 (the World Development 2006 version is titled 1984-2003 and was not accessed). Read: abstract, introduction and the sections on the two indexes and rankings (about half).
- WHY: is aid targeted to good institutions and to poverty? HOW: 41 donor agencies; a policy-selectivity index (preference for better institutions given income and population) and a poverty-selectivity index (preference for poorer countries given institutions), estimated per donor, tested with the World Bank CPIA and with independent institutional measures. WHAT: the same donors are high on both indexes (IDA, Denmark, UK, Norway, Ireland, Netherlands); France and the US are not particularly selective; Japan is policy-selective but not poverty-selective; selectivity rose from 1984-89 to the 1990s. The authors note that "ideal weights" on poverty and policy do not exist in principle, so they report two indexes rather than a combined one.
- Relevance: a statement in the literature that donors hold different preference weights over poverty and policy, which is the object inverse GP estimates, and a cautionary example of the weighting ambiguity.

### 5. Collier and Dollar, "Aid Allocation and Poverty Reduction"
- April 1999 draft (Stanford course reading copy, World Bank Development Research Group); published in European Economic Review 46(8), 1475-1500, 2002, DOI 10.1016/s0014-2921(01)00187-8 (Crossref). Read: abstract, introduction and the sections deriving the poverty-efficient allocation; the published version was not accessed.
- WHY: derive the allocation that maximises poverty reduction and compare it with actual aid. HOW: stage one, a growth regression with an aid-policy interaction and diminishing returns (using World Bank policy ratings); stage two, a mapping from growth to poverty reduction using the poverty headcount and an income elasticity of poverty; the allocator maximises total poverty reduction subject to a total aid budget, giving first-order conditions that equalise the marginal cost of lifting a person out of poverty across countries (shadow price of aid). WHAT: actual allocation differs sharply from the efficient one: in the efficient allocation aid tapers in with policy reform at a given poverty level, in the actual allocation it tapers out; the draft abstract says countries with severe poverty and adequate policies hold 75 percent of the world's poor but receive 53 percent of aid.
- Relevance: the only constrained-optimisation benchmark I read that is a genuine optimisation treatment of aid. It gives a closed-form "efficient" allocation (shadow price lambda) that can serve as one goal's target, and shows the dependence on an estimated outcome function, which is not identified from allocation data alone (see threats).

### 6. Leo, "Inside the World Bank's Black Box Allocation System: How Well Does IDA Allocate Resources to the Neediest and Most Vulnerable Countries?"
- Center for Global Development, June 2010 (SSRN DOI 10.2139/ssrn.1646613 per Crossref). Read: abstract, the full description of the PBA steps, and the headline results (about 70 percent of the text).
- WHY: assess how need-focused IDA's formula is. HOW: a step-by-step replay of the IDA-15 performance-based allocation (PBA) and a comparison of per-capita allocations across need and vulnerability quartiles. WHAT, formula as described in this 2010 paper (IDA-15 era; later replenishments differ): Country Performance Rating = 0.24 x mean(CPIA clusters A to C) + 0.68 x CPIA cluster D + 0.08 x portfolio quality; allocation score = CPR^5.0 x population^1.0 x GNI per capita^(-0.125); relative share = score / sum of scores, times the envelope; then exceptions: base allocation per country (SDR 1.5 million in IDA-15), per-capita caps (SDR 19.8 per capita for non-post-conflict countries), and special treatment for post-conflict countries and large blend countries (without a cap India would take about 60 percent of IDA-15 resources). Result: only two of eight need and vulnerability measures reach parity between top and bottom quartile; exceptions more than double per-capita allocations to the neediest.
- Relevance: a published, known allocation rule, which is the best ground truth for an inverse method: the exponents (5.0, 1.0, -0.125), the caps and the floor are known, so recovered weights can be checked exactly. Caps and floors are the kinks that make targets and thresholds identifiable. The exponent on income is tiny (-0.125) and on performance large (5.0): an inverse method should recover this ordering.

### 7. Fleck and Kilby, "Changing Aid Regimes? U.S. Foreign Aid from the Cold War to the War on Terror"
- Villanova Working Paper 1, version dated 13 June 2008; published Journal of Development Economics 91(2), 185-197, 2010, DOI 10.1016/j.jdeveco.2009.09.011 (Crossref). Read: abstract, introduction, literature and data sections (about half); the published version was not accessed.
- WHY: did the War on Terror reduce the weight on need in US aid? HOW: US aid budget analysis 1955-2006 controlling for domestic politics, plus a panel of aid to 119 countries over the same period with need and donor-interest variables and period interactions; the authors list estimators in use (log with constant, Tobit, Heckman, two-part). WHAT: US aid increased, including for the poorest countries, after 2001; the emphasis on need had risen for 35 years and then fell for core recipients during the War on Terror.
- Relevance: a time-varying-weights revealed-preference result for a single donor with a clear regime change, which is what inverse GP per period would try to reproduce; shows that budget size and allocation shares need to be modelled together (the envelope varies).

### 8. White, Berrett, Neeley-Tass and Findley, "Modeling Efficiency of Foreign Aid Allocation in Malawi"
- arXiv 1608.03024v2 [stat.AP], 2017. Read: abstract and introduction (not the model details).
- WHY: is aid within Malawi allocated to need? HOW: Bayesian spatial generalised linear mixed models relating about 2,500 geocoded aid projects since 2003 to economic indicators in seven sectors; the best model (spatial gamma regression) is used to derive a "more efficient" scenario and compare it to the actual allocation. WHAT: finds sector-level evidence on efficiency and provides a comparison scenario.
- Relevance: a within-recipient, sector-level data source (Open Aid Malawi) and a statistical, not optimisation, notion of efficiency; useful as a subnational ground-truth domain but outside the donor-by-recipient data collected here.

### 9. Sakr, Selim and Taha, "Measuring countries' relative efficiencies in using development assistance: a data envelopment analysis approach"
- Future Business Journal 10(1), 2024, article 40, DOI 10.1186/s43093-024-00325-5, open access. Read: abstract, introduction and the start of the literature review; methods and results not read in full.
- WHY: how efficiently do recipients convert aid into SDG outcomes. HOW: seven DEA (linear programming) models for 86 countries, 2002-2020, with ODA as input and three SDG indicators as outputs; bootstrapping for robustness. WHAT: efficiency often falls short of optimal, especially in low-income countries.
- Relevance: an operations-research treatment of the recipient side (production frontier), a possible source for outcome weights, and an example that the OR literature on aid focuses on efficiency, not on revealed donor preferences.

### 10. Munoz-Ocana, Torres Jimenez, Carbonero-Ruz and Pacheco-Martinez, "A Multi-criteria optimization proposal for aid allocation: combining donor and recipient interests"
- Revista de Economia Mundial, issue 62, 2022, pp. 151-170, DOI 10.33776/rem.vi62.6972 (Crossref). Read: the Crossref abstract only; the PDF could not be downloaded (server error), so nothing about the model details is claimed.
- From the abstract: a tool combining donor and recipient objectives using concentration curves and Suits indices to determine an optimal distribution of aid through a constrained optimisation programme, usable at donor level and for donor coordination.
- Relevance: the nearest forward model I found (multi-criteria, donor and recipient objectives). It is a normative design tool, not an estimator from observed data. Details to be read.

## Synthesis

### What is known
1. Reduced-form evidence is consistent: both need and donor interest matter, colonial and strategic links are large, donors differ strongly (Alesina and Dollar; Berthelemy; Hoeffler and Outram; Dollar and Levin). The Hoeffler-Outram decomposition says donor fixed effects dominate, then need, then self-interest, with merit marginal.
2. Time variation exists: Dollar and Levin (selectivity up from the 1980s to the 1990s) and Fleck and Kilby (US need weight falling during the War on Terror).
3. Known rules exist: IDA's PBA formula (Leo) has explicit exponents, a base allocation and per-capita caps; Collier and Dollar give a closed-form optimal rule from an estimated outcome function. These are the optimisation-flavoured pieces.
4. Optimisation has been used for aid mainly on the efficiency or design side (Collier-Dollar, DEA, the multi-criteria proposal), not for inferring donor priorities. The only inverse-optimisation paper found is Karami-Fard (2026; abstract only).

### What inverse GP could add
- Targets as well as weights. Donors state targets (0.7 percent of GNI, 0.15 to 0.20 percent to LDCs, EU interim targets, the UK statute), which are aspiration levels in the GP sense; Karami-Fard's abstract recovers weights only.
- Deviation structure: under-achievement of a stated target is penalised, over-achievement is not; this matches how aid pledges behave (in the DAC1 series for 2010-2024 Norway and Luxembourg stay at or above about 0.86 to 1.1 percent, Denmark falls from 0.91 to 0.67-0.73, Germany rises from 0.37 to 0.85 in 2022 and falls to 0.68 in 2024, the US stays at 0.15-0.24) and creates kinks that identify targets.
- A set-valued output (the cone of weights and targets consistent with observed shares) which suits the finding that donor fixed effects and decision noise are large.
- Budget variation: yearly ODA/GNI changes (the UK drop from 0.70 in 2020 to 0.50 in 2021 in the DAC1 data) shift the envelope while the preference stays fixed, a natural experiment for identification.

### Identification threats
- Collinearity and the fixed-effect problem: need (low income), colonial ties and commercial links are strongly correlated; Hoeffler and Outram show that donor-specific constants explain half of predicted aid.
- Selection and zeros: many donor-recipient pairs are not served; DAC2A does not distinguish zero from unreported; negative net disbursements exist (4,870 in the core panel).
- Objective proxies: the weights depend on the chosen measures of need, alignment and commerce; the outcome function (Collier-Dollar) is not identified from allocation data.
- Multilateral channels: the DAC2A series is bilateral; a donor can express priorities through core contributions to IDA or UN agencies, which are not attributed to recipients here.
- Commitments versus disbursements: much of the literature uses commitments; the series here are disbursements, which lag decisions.
- Structural breaks: ODA/GNI measure changes in 2018 (grant-equivalent basis); donor lists and recipient classifications change over time (LDC status is a snapshot).
- Weight non-uniqueness: with vertex solutions or proportional shares, weights are generically not point-identified, consistent with the theory notes in `05_inverse_optimization_theory.md` and `08_G4_synthesis.md`.

### Ground-truth options (ranked by strength)
1. IDA PBA formula (Leo 2010 describes IDA-15): known exponents, base allocation and caps. Needs IDA allocation by country and CPIA scores (not collected here; check World Bank sources).
2. UK statutory 0.7 percent target, breached in 2021 (0.50 percent) with a stated plan to reach 0.3 percent by 2027; DAC1 series shows 0.70 (2020), 0.50 (2021), 0.51 (2022). Source URLs in `targets.csv`.
3. EU Council conclusions of 24 May 2005 (0.56 percent collective by 2010, 0.51 percent individual, 0.17 percent for post-2002 members, 0.7 percent by 2015, 0.33 percent for post-2002 members by 2015): donor-group-specific targets with years; text read.
4. UN 0.7 percent and 0.15-0.20 percent to LDCs (SDG target 17.2; read at sdgs.un.org). The LDC share can be computed from DAC2A (the `LDC` aggregate is present; its 2020 value for Germany equals the sum over the 44 listed LDCs).
5. Norway's national 1 percent goal: only a search-result summary was seen (source page returned 403); verify before use.
6. Donor-specific need/commerce typologies from Berthelemy and Karami-Fard (abstract level) as weak external checks of recovered weight rankings (Nordics at the need pole, France and Japan at the commerce pole).
