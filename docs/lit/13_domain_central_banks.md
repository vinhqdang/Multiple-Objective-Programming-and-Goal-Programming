# Domain note 13: central banks as baseline / ground-truth domain (2026-11-22)

Scope. Eight papers read (full text for six, abstract/introduction or partial reading for two), all retrieved in this session (PDF text extracted and checked against the numbers quoted below). ## Per-paper notes

### 1. Dennis (2006), "The policy preferences of the US Federal Reserve"
- Citation: J. Applied Econometrics 21(1):55-77, DOI 10.1002/jae.808. Read: FRBSF WP 2001-08 (July 2004), https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/wp01-08bk.pdf, DOI 10.24148/wp2001-08; full text. Published version not read.
- WHY: estimated Taylor rules are reduced forms; they do not identify the objective or the implicit inflation target (Sec. 1, and Sec. 2: a vertical long-run Phillips curve means the target cannot be identified from the Phillips curve alone).
- HOW: small backward-looking IS and Phillips curves (Rudebusch-Svensson type) plus the first-order conditions of a discounted LQ problem, loss = sum beta^j [(pi - pi*)^2 + lambda y^2 + nu (di)^2], estimated jointly by quasi-FIML; weight on inflation normalised to 1, targets for output gap and rate change set to zero; beta calibrated (0.99).
- WHAT: 1982Q1-2000Q2: lambda = 2.94 (s.e. 5.69, insignificant), nu = 4.52 (1.75), pi* = 1.38, r* = 1.66. Varying beta from 0.95 to 1.00 moves pi* between 1.33 and 1.56 and the weights along with it (lambda 1.16 to 4.23, nu 1.69 to 6.56; Table 4). 1966Q1-1979Q3: pi* = 6.96, weights insignificant (lambda 3.14 s.e. 8.2, nu 37.2 s.e. 57.8), author says the weights are "only weakly identified" and the sample spans three chairmen. Identification: cross-equation restrictions from optimality; the target enters through the policy equation's intercept, jointly with r*.

### 2. Favero & Rovelli (2003), "Macroeconomic stability and the preferences of the Fed: a formal analysis, 1961-98"
- Citation: J. Money, Credit and Banking 35(4):545-556, DOI 10.1353/mcb.2003.0028. Read: IGIER WP 200 (2001), https://repec.unibocconi.it/igier/igi/wp/2001/200.pdf; full text (the PDF's font encoding is a Caesar shift and was decoded). Published version not read.
- WHY: separate shifts in Fed preferences, smaller supply-shock variance and more efficient policy as explanations for lower inflation (4.5% in 1960-79 to 3.6% in 1980-98).
- HOW: structural IS and Phillips curve estimated jointly with the Euler equations (GMM) of an intertemporal loss with inflation target pi*, output weight lambda, rate-smoothing weight nu (inflation weight = 1), discount factor fixed at 0.975. Single-equation Taylor rules are shown not to identify (pi*, beta, lambda) (Sec. 2).
- WHAT: 1961:1-1979:2: pi* = 5.795 (s.e. 0.07), lambda = 0.00153, nu = 0.0051; 1980:3-1998:3: pi* = 2.628 (0.06), lambda = 0.00125, nu = 0.0085 (all weights "small but clearly significant", per the text). The main change across eras is pi*, 5.8% to 2.6%, mirrored by implied equilibrium real rate 1.9% to 3.7%. The tiny standard errors are conditional on the fixed beta and on correct model specification; scale of weights depends on their units, so not comparable with Dennis.

### 3. Ozlale (2003), "Price stability vs. output stability: tales of Federal Reserve administrations"
- Citation: J. Economic Dynamics and Control 27(9):1595-1610, DOI 10.1016/s0165-1889(02)00072-6. Read: working paper "Tales from three Federal Reserve administrations", 11 Jan 2002, https://content.csbs.utah.edu/~ehrbar/erc2002/pdf/P106.pdf; full text. Published version not read.
- HOW: inflation and output equations jointly with the optimal linear regulator for loss lambda_pi pi^2 + lambda_y y^2 + lambda_i (di)^2 with weights summing to one and targets set to zero (so no inflation target is estimated).
- WHAT (Table 5): Burns 1970:1-1978:1 (0.33, 0.37, 0.30); Volcker 1979:3-1987:2 (0.45, 0.21, 0.34); Greenspan 1987:3-1999:1 (0.42, 0.22, 0.36); whole sample (0.39, 0.26, 0.35). Ratio lambda_pi/lambda_y: 0.89, 2.14, 1.91. A structural break at the Volcker appointment is reported. Normalising weights to sum to one instead of inflation = 1 is a different identifying convention, and the zero targets are an assumption, not a finding.

### 4. Ilbas (2010), "Estimation of monetary policy preferences in a forward-looking model: a Bayesian approach"
- Citation: Int. J. Central Banking 6(3):169-209 (no DOI found in Crossref). Read: NBB Working Paper 129 (March 2008), https://www.nbb.be/doc/ts/publications/wp/wp129en.pdf; full text. Published version not read.
- HOW: Smets-Wouters (2003) euro-area DSGE model with its Taylor rule replaced by optimal commitment (timeless perspective, 40-quarter presample) under a quadratic loss in inflation, output gap and rate change (model M1), plus wage inflation (M2); inflation weight normalised to 1; weights estimated jointly with structural parameters by Bayesian methods with priors.
- WHAT: q_y = 0.0401 (M1), 0.04 (M2); q_r = 0.6309 (M1), 0.624 (M2); wage-inflation weight 0.269 in M2, but marginal likelihood prefers M1 (q_w = 0). Footnote 27: q_y becomes about 0.64 once converted to annual inflation and rates. Inflation variability is the main concern. The optimal-policy model fits only slightly worse (marginal likelihood) than the original Taylor-rule model. No inflation target is estimated (inflation is detrended around its sample mean). Priors and the commitment assumption partly identify the weights; the paper reports sensitivity of the weights to the treatment of forward-looking agents.

### 5. Ireland (2007), "Changes in the Federal Reserve's inflation target: causes and consequences"
- Citation: J. Money, Credit and Banking 39(8):1851-1882. Read: NBER WP 12492 (Aug 2006), DOI 10.3386/w12492, https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12492/w12492.pdf; full text. Published version not read.
- WHY/HOW: New Keynesian model with an unobserved inflation target that follows a random walk; maximum likelihood on 1959:1-2004:2 quarterly US data; identification rests on the assumption that permanent changes in inflation need corresponding target changes (the random-walk target is called an identifying assumption). There is no loss function; the target enters the Taylor rule.
- WHAT: estimated target (endogenous-target variant) 1.24% in 1959:1, peaks 8.52% (1974:4) and 8.12% (1980:4), low 1.80% (1998:2, 2002:1), 2.48% in 2004:2; exogenous-target variant 1.18, 7.98, 8.02, 1.70, 2.14. Author notes considerable uncertainty about the source of target shifts.

### 6. Shapiro & Wilson (2021), "Taking the Fed at its word: a new approach to estimating central bank objectives using text analysis"
- Citation: FRBSF Working Paper 2019-02 (Jan 2021), DOI 10.24148/wp2019-02, https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/wp2019-02.pdf. Read: full text (I did not check whether a journal version exists).
- WHY/HOW: avoids a structural model and avoids observing rate actions: regress the sentiment (negativity) of FOMC meeting speech, measured by lexicon-based text analysis, on Greenbook forecasts, and read the loss-minimising inflation rate off the fitted loss. Quadratic (U-shaped), symmetric piecewise-linear (V-shaped) and asymmetric piecewise-linear losses (eq. 5, with delta_1 and delta_2 slopes above and below pi*, estimated by grid search over pi* in [0, 3] step 0.01) are used.
- WHAT: 2000-2011 baseline: quadratic pi* about 1.25% (95% CI 0.8-1.7); V-shaped about 1.4 to 1.55; asymmetric results about 1.5; text says a target of 2% fits worse. Narrative analysis of stated FOMC preferences found a consensus of 1.5% for most of the period, with some members at 2% during the Great Recession; subsamples by narrative preference gave implied targets close to 1.5 and close to 2. The official 2% target was announced in January 2012, after the sample. The authors flag identification issues for rate-based approaches: only periods with rate changes are informative, and the zero lower bound (2008-2015, from March 2020) and quarter-point increments constrain policy.

### 7. Montes-Rojas, Toledo, Bertholet & Corfield (2025), "Implicit quantile preferences of the Fed and the Taylor rule"
- Citation: arXiv:2510.24362 (28 Oct 2025), https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.24362. Read: abstract, introduction, data and calibration sections, conclusions and summary of results; the derivation sections were skimmed, not checked.
- HOW: central bank maximises a quantile (not expected) utility; Euler equations give a Taylor-type rule; the quantile index tau is backed out by indirect inference, on FRED quarterly data 1954-Q4 to 2025-Q2 (283 observations). The inflation target is calibrated, not estimated: 2% annual (0.496% quarterly).
- WHAT: the Fed is mostly "dovish" (high tau) with hawkish episodes (lower tau around the early 1980s). No weights or targets are estimated, so it is relevant mainly as a case where the preference parameter is risk attitude and the target is imposed.

### 8. Lopez-Salido, Markowitz & Nelson (2024), "Continuity and change in the Federal Reserve's perspective on price stability"
- Citation: FEDS 2024-041, DOI 10.17016/FEDS.2024.041, https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2024041pap.pdf. Read: abstract and introduction only.
- WHAT: from policymakers' statements since the early 1950s the authors conclude that periods of high inflation should not be read as a belief in the desirability of inflation. Relevance: an implied target of 7% in the 1970s (Dennis, Ireland) reflects a model-based inference that conflicts with the statements; it is evidence on how an inferred "target" can differ from the intent.

Not retrieved (so not cited as read): Clarida, Gali & Gertler (2000) (the NBER PDF was an image scan), Bordo & Istrefi (NBER w24650; only its title page was extracted), Castelnuovo, Salemi, Givens, Lakdawala (2016, abstract seen via search only), Surico, Aguiar & Martins, Taylor (1993). For inverse optimisation in general see notes 05 and 06 in this folder; they were not re-read here.

## Synthesis

**What is known.** (i) Estimated Taylor rules are reduced forms that do not identify preferences (Dennis, Favero-Rovelli, Shapiro-Wilson all argue this). (ii) Structural approaches recover (lambda, nu, pi*) by imposing the first-order conditions of an LQ problem and estimating them jointly with a macro model; the inflation weight is normalised to 1 (Dennis, Favero-Rovelli, Ilbas) or weights sum to 1 (Ozlale). (iii) Results are fragile: the output-gap weight is insignificant in Dennis, "small but significant" in Favero-Rovelli, 0.04 to 0.64 depending on the frequency convention in Ilbas; the smoothing weight is the most robust feature; Dennis's weights move by a factor of 3 to 4 as the discount factor changes from 0.95 to 1. (iv) Implicit post-1980 US inflation targets: 1.38 (Dennis, 1982-2000), 2.63 (Favero-Rovelli, 1980-98), 1.25 to 1.55 (Shapiro-Wilson, 2000-2011), 2.1 to 2.5 in 2004 (Ireland); the official target (2%, announced January 2012) was not stated in any of those sample periods. A spread of about 1.3 percentage points across methods for one bank and era is the empirical reference for how precisely an implicit target can be recovered. (v) Pre-Volcker implicit targets (5.8 to 8.5) are reported in several papers but the weights in that period are weakly identified (Dennis).

**Goal-programming inverse model versus quadratic loss.** Notation: deviations d+_i, d-_i from aspiration level b_i, weights w_i; minimise sum of weighted deviations (or max, Chebyshev) subject to the policy constraints.
- A quadratic loss gives linear decision rules and a smooth map from preferences to decisions; the target pi* appears only as an intercept (Dennis) and is therefore jointly identified with r*. A linear (L1) goal-programming loss gives piecewise-linear, kinked decisions; the aspiration level enters through which deviation variable is active. Shapiro-Wilson's V-shaped and asymmetric specifications (eq. 5) are exactly one-goal GP with different weights on d+ and d-, which makes the asymmetry (e.g. a higher cost of undershooting at the zero lower bound) estimable.
- Dennis's loss has a zero target for the output gap and rate change; GP would allow aspirations on those too (e.g. a non-zero gap goal), which cannot be separated from the intercept in a quadratic model without extra variation.
- In GP the weights and aspiration levels are only set-identified in general (see note 08: vertex optima, targets informative only through kinks; in Chebyshev GP weights and targets are confounded), whereas the structural quadratic estimators obtain point estimates by assuming the macro model and fixing beta, at the price of the fragility above. The inverse GP output should therefore be reported as a set (cone) and compared with these point estimates, and one should expect the set to be wide for banks that were always close to target.
- Quadratic-loss estimators work with the model's shocks as the stochastic part; inverse GP as planned in this project works on observed decisions with noise. Taylor-rule estimates are the natural reduced-form baseline against which any extra identification should be shown.

**Using stated targets as ground truth.** The `targets.csv` table gives numeric targets, with source URLs, for banks and dates; the plan is: (1) fit the inverse model on windows after the adoption date and test whether the recovered aspiration for inflation contains the stated value (for example the Fed after 2012-01, ECB 2003-2021 versus after 2021-07, Norges Bank before and after 2018-03, Sweden around 2017, South Africa before and after 2025-11, Brazil 2025 onward); (2) use changes of the stated target as a natural experiment: the method should detect a shift from 2.5 to 2.0 in Norway in March 2018 and from 2.5 RPIX to 2 CPI in the UK in 2004, and should not show one where there was none; (3) use bands (Canada, Australia, Brazil, India, Mexico, Poland) to check whether the weight on the inflation goal rises when inflation leaves the band; (4) report bias relative to the stated value: Shapiro-Wilson's 1.25 to 1.55 against a stated 2 shows that an unbiased recovery is not guaranteed, since a revealed target can legitimately differ from the announced one. Caveat on the table itself: eight rows rest on pages fetched and read, the others on search-result summaries and must be re-verified before they serve as ground truth (README).

**Threats.**
- Forward-looking behaviour: Dennis's backward-looking constraints give a target that depends on the model; Ilbas shows the smoothing weight depends on whether agents are forward looking, and that commitment versus discretion changes the interpretation; policy rates respond to forecasts, so real-time (Greenbook, staff) forecasts rather than ex-post data are needed (Shapiro-Wilson). The data here are final-vintage, so the first-stage test will be biased by data revisions.
- Zero lower bound and discrete steps: only periods with rate changes identify the loss (Shapiro-Wilson); during the ZLB (2008-2015 and from March 2020 for the US, per Shapiro-Wilson) observed decisions are corner solutions, so the inverse model must treat the rate constraint as binding (a kink, which GP can handle explicitly) or drop those windows; also unconventional instruments are outside the policy-rate data.
- Model misspecification and weak identification: weights depend on the discount factor (Dennis Table 4), on units/normalisation (Ilbas footnote 27; Ozlale's sum-to-one), on priors (Ilbas), and on assumed constant targets (Dennis notes this is hard to reconcile with the 1970s data; Ireland lets the target drift as a random walk). The sample spans several chairs, so regimes are mixed.
- Inferred versus intended targets: model-implied 1970s targets near 7 to 8% conflict with stated policymaker beliefs (Lopez-Salido et al.), and inflation-targeting banks differ in the targeted index (PCE, HICP, CPIF), bands and horizons (medium term), which the BIS headline CPI series does not match exactly.
- Committee aggregation and politics: the objective is that of a committee; targets are sometimes set by governments (UK, South Africa, India) and the bank may weigh other objectives (financial stability, exchange rate for EM banks) that are omitted from a three-goal model.
