# Referee report: "Revealed Aspiration Levels: Inverse Interval Goal Programming for Recovering the Targets and Priorities Behind Public Decisions"

Submission to the Annals of Operations Research, special issue "Multiple Objective Programming and Goal Programming: AI for Decision Making in Economic and Social Sciences".

Basis of this report: the .tex source (main_sn.tex and the included tab_*.tex files), plus spot checks against results/*.csv and docs/09_preregistration_confirmatory_cb.md. I did not rerun any computation. Numbers I recomputed from the tables are labelled as such.

**Recommendation: major revision.** The structural results for the one-goal case are mostly correct and neat, and the author is unusually candid about negative findings. But (a) several identification statements are stated more strongly than proved, (b) the central-bank "validation" does not support the headline claim once a trivial benchmark is added and one outlier is removed, (c) the multi-goal part, which is the part that concerns *multiple* objectives, has neither identification theory nor a verified optimiser, and (d) the aid "goal-seeking" test cannot distinguish goal programs from generic state-dependent behaviour.

---

## 1. Claimed contributions and novelty

Claimed contributions (Introduction, p. 2):
1. A forward model of repeated interval goal programming with adjustment cost, and a change of variables that linearises the inverse problem for given discrete states.
2. Structural results: breakpoint optimality, effective weights, edge revelation, non-identifiability of weights under linear cost, and a rank condition under quadratic cost.
3. Estimation: closed-form one-goal policy, an exact "O(T^4)" estimator with a certificate, an exact MILP and a multistart heuristic for K>=2, and a consistency theorem.
4. A "pre-registered" validation on central banks, and applications to aid, defence and firms.

**Novelty assessment.**
- The statement "we found no method that recovers aspiration levels and goal weights jointly from noisy sequential decisions" (p. 2) is too strong. The one-goal quadratic-cost policy of Theorem 1 is a dead-zone-plus-saturation rule. It is the standard "inaction band / opportunistic" structure and, for a central bank, the opportunistic-disinflation and asymmetric-loss literature. The relevant lines include Orphanides and Wilcox (2002), Ruge-Murcia (2003), Surico (2007) and Kilian and Manganelli (2008, implicit target range under asymmetric loss), and the (S,s) / threshold-regression literature. Please verify and cite the exact references. Kilian and Manganelli in particular estimate an implicit inflation target *band* and asymmetric weights from central-bank behaviour. The paper cites only Dennis, Favero-Rovelli, Ozlale, Ireland and Shapiro in this area.
- On the inverse-optimization side, online and sequential inverse optimization and inverse MPC / inverse optimal control (dynamically revealed preferences) are not discussed.
- The genuine novelty is the packaging: an OR-style exact algorithm for the one-goal estimator, plus the identification results. That is a reasonable contribution, but it is a *one-goal* contribution. For K=1 "goal programming" is a single-objective problem with a dead-zone loss, and the "multiple objective / priority" content that the special issue asks about is carried only by the K>=2 heuristic. The heuristic has no identification or consistency theory (see 3 and 4).
- "Priorities" in the title and abstract are not priorities in the GP sense (lexicographic or relative importance). They are effective step sizes omega measured in units of the adjustment cost (see Major 3).

## 2. Correctness of propositions and theorems

I checked each proof step. Items marked **[gap]** need repair. Items marked **[ok]** are correct as stated.

**Proposition 1 (Breakpoints)** [ok, minor]. F_t >= |D| uses lambda=1 and C=|D|, fine. The claim "a strict minimiser cannot lie in the interior of an affine piece" is right. Minor: the existence statement and the kink set are fine, but note that the proposition says nothing about *which* kink, so its use in the MILP relies on all 2K+1 candidates.

**Proposition 2 (Normalisation / effective weights)** [ok but overstated]. The algebra (omega = w|beta|, alpha = 1/|beta|) checks. However:
- "Decisions depend on the weights only through omega" is true, but decisions also depend on (alpha, c, d). The sentence "w_k is identified whenever alpha_k and omega_k are" is a tautology. The practical consequence is that the *priority* w_k is identified only if the transmission alpha_k is, and Proposition 3 / 5 never establish this for K>=2.
- Because lambda is normalised to 1, omega is a ratio of goal weight to adjustment-cost weight and carries the units of the policy instrument. Cross-goal or cross-actor comparisons of omega are meaningless unless the units of D are common. The abstract's "priority weights" should be rephrased.

**Proposition 3 (Edge revelation)** [ok, with a caveat]. The linear-equation argument is correct. The caveat is the hypothesis "the binding goal and edge are known". In estimation they are latent, and the paper never shows that this knowledge can be recovered from data for K>=2. The proposition therefore does not deliver identification of (alpha, tau, b) for the central-bank K=2 application. Add a statement of exactly which latent-state patterns are distinguishable, or drop the claim of identification for K>=2.

**Proposition 4 (Weights never point-identified under linear cost)** [gap].
- (a) "A nonempty open polyhedron, so it contains a neighbourhood of any of its points". The constraint omega >= 0 is closed, so the set is open only relative to the orthant. At a point with some omega_k = 0 it does not contain a full neighbourhood. The conclusion (not a singleton) survives, but the stated reason is imprecise. Fix: say "relatively open in the orthant and of dimension >= 1 whenever nonempty".
- (b) The proof treats the observed decisions as exact strict minimisers (noiseless). With noise the object of study is a decision-space loss minimiser, and non-uniqueness is then a statement about the *population* argmin set Theta_I of Theorem 5. Say so.
- (c) "Never" is too strong without the strictness assumption: with weak optimality the set is a closed polyhedron and could in principle be a point at a degenerate vertex. State that the result is generic.
- (d) The motivation for the paper's pivot to quadratic cost is therefore: linear cost gives no point identification of weights, quadratic does. But the choice of quadratic cost is then also driven by what the data fit (Section 6 development paragraph: linear-cost specifications failed to beat the benchmark). There is no test of linear against quadratic adjustment cost on the same data. State this explicitly as a modelling choice, not a finding about behaviour.

**Proposition 5 (Weights under gradualism)** [gap; the "if and only if" is false as stated].
- The proof shows that the periods with no edge goals give linear equations D*_t = sum_A omega+ - sum_B omega-. Full column rank of that incidence matrix is *sufficient* for point identification. It is not *necessary*, because periods with goals on an edge contribute inequalities (multipliers in [0, omega_k]), which can pin down a weight that has no equation of its own (for example omega_k^+ >= pi >= D*_t gives a one-sided bound, and combined with other bounds can give a point). Conversely the incidence matrix has a zero column for any omega that never appears, but the inequalities may still identify it. Fix: replace "if and only if" by "if", or state the exact necessary and sufficient condition including the edge-period inequalities.
- The statement is conditional on (alpha, c, d) being known *and* on the sets A_t, B_t being observable. These sets depend on the unknown (alpha, c, d). The proposition therefore does not show joint identification of aspiration levels and weights. The Remark following it asserts it ("Weights and aspiration levels together are therefore identified when the data contain both single-goal adjustment periods and edge-stopping periods") without proof. Either prove it, at least for K=1 where Theorem 5(iv) already does, or label it a heuristic.
- The abstract says priority weights "are point-identified under a rank condition on the observed adjustment episodes". That is conditional on the other parameters being known, so it overstates what is proved.

**Theorem 1 (Closed-form policy)** [ok]. I rechecked the derivative cases (u = alpha x - e+, l = alpha x - e-) and the boundary cases u = omega+, u = 0, l <= 0; the clip formula is correct, and the claim that the two terms are never simultaneously nonzero and that the policy is 1-Lipschitz in z = alpha x is correct. Minor: the proof says "the cases l <= 0 are symmetric" without handling u >= 0 and l <= 0 simultaneously (impossible unless u=l=0, fine; say so).

Conceptual consequence not drawn by the author: the policy in Theorem 1 is "move to the band edge, but by at most omega per period". That is observationally identical to a rule with a *hard rate limit* |Delta| <= omega, or to a menu-cost / inaction-region rule. The paper's own placebo "quadratic program with |Delta| <= 0.35" (Table 6, row 4) shows the fitted goal program fits it with 71% gain over holding. So the "weights" are not separable from institutional constraints on step size. See Major 3.

**Theorem 2 (Exact estimation for fixed alpha, O(T^4))** [correct but loosely proved; complexity claim true but not tight].
- I checked the hyperplane arrangement and the vertex enumeration in the proof. The four-hyperplane vertices in the product arrangement give e+ in E and omega+ in {D_s} union {z_t - e+} union {0}, and the lower family is the mirror image; the coupling e- = e+ case is covered. The prefix-sum decomposition A(e+) - B(e-) of the dead-zone part is correct, and the bound omega <= max D argument is correct. So the algorithm returns the global optimum for fixed alpha.
- Gaps: (i) "a vertex is the intersection of four independent hyperplanes" relies on the cells being bounded polytopes. This needs the explicit box on e (stated only as "a bounded interval", and "plus the interval bounds" adds the bounds to E in an unspecified way). State the box and show that active bounds cannot be optimal for a sufficiently wide box, or include them explicitly in the candidate set of Algorithm 1. (ii) Algorithm 1, lines 3-4: the candidate sets {D_s} and {-D_s} must be restricted to nonnegative values (omega >= 0). (iii) Degenerate arrangements (ties, parallel hyperplanes) are not addressed; harmless but one sentence is needed.
- Complexity: the count is |E| = O(T^2) values of e, times O(T) candidates for omega, times O(T) to evaluate the sum, i.e. O(T^4). This is a valid upper bound, but the inner minimisation over omega for fixed e can be done by sorting and sweeping in O(T log T), giving O(T^3 log T). The paper's own timings (Table 4: x5 per doubling of T up to x7.5) indicate exponent about 2.3-2.9, consistent with that. The abstract and theorem should say "O(T^4) (O(T^3 log T) with a sweep)". Also state the memory cost.
- The claim "polynomial time ... whose optimality is certified" in the abstract applies only to K=1 and fixed alpha (or alpha searched on a grid, see Theorem 3).

**Theorem 3 (Certified search over alpha)** [ok with qualifications]. The Lipschitz argument (each residual changes by at most |x_t||alpha - alpha'|, minimum over an alpha-independent domain preserves it) is correct, and the L eta/2 bound for the best grid point is correct. Qualifications: (i) the domain of e must be fixed independently of alpha, which has to be specified (it is not, and it interacts with the box in Theorem 2); (ii) the certificate is on the loss, as the paper says, but the economic outputs are tau = c/alpha and b = d/alpha, and a small loss gap can coexist with large tau differences (the UK aid example: the optimal loss with tau = 0.27 vs the heuristic's 0.49). The "certified" label in the abstract should be qualified accordingly.

**Theorem 4 (Consistency)** [essentially correct under its assumptions, but the assumptions do not fit the applications].
- Step (i) (unique minimiser of E|eps + a| at the median) and steps (ii)-(iii) (Holder-1/2 continuity of the minimiser in the parameters, an integrable envelope, ergodic ULLN) are fine. The identification step (iv) for K=1 is fine, provided that omega_0^+ > 0 and omega_0^- > 0 and that the x-distribution has support over all kinks.
- (B1) requires {(x_t, eps_t)} to be strictly stationary and ergodic. In the aid application, x_t is the previous level and x_{t+1} = x_t + D_t, so x is a controlled process fed back by the noise. Stationarity and ergodicity of that closed loop (and of the placebo's "decision added to the state") is an assumption on the *dynamics*, not a primitive. It is not verified, and the permutation test explicitly builds random-walk level paths. State a sufficient condition (e.g. contractivity of the closed loop) or restrict the theorem to exogenous x.
- (B2) requires the *conditional* median of the noise to be zero and a positive density near zero. Central-bank decisions are discrete (multiples of 25 bp, many zeros), so a density does not exist; the theorem does not cover the data actually used. Say so.
- Consistency for K>=2: the "support condition" part of the theorem is only for K=1. The central-bank application is K=2, so *no* identification or consistency statement covers it, and the heuristic is not guaranteed to find the minimiser the theorem concerns. The abstract's "we prove that the estimator is consistent for the identified set" should say "for the exact minimiser of the decision-space loss".
- The envelope claim |D*| <= sum_k max(omega_k^+, omega_k^-) is correct.
- Compactness of Theta (B3) is violated in practice: the estimator searches data-dependent ranges (the paper says so after the Monte Carlo), and the central-bank fits hit the lower bound of alpha (bootstrap section), which is exactly where compactness matters.

**Theorem 5 (Misspecification)** [ok]. The argument (steps (ii)-(iii) do not use the model equation) is correct. But the interpretation paragraph undersells the consequence: Theta* can be non-unique, and the fitted band b is not interpretable. In the placebo, partial adjustment (which has no band at all) yields median b_hat = 0.29 and the QP rule yields 0.26 (misspec_placebo.csv). So b_hat is an artefact of the functional form when behaviour is smooth. The paper reports bands for all applications without this warning.

**MILP formulation** (Section 5) [correct in form, unverified in effect]. I rechecked the one-sided derivative inequalities (right derivative -sum omega+ s + sum omega- l + 1 - 2v >= eps; left derivative -sum omega+ a + sum omega- z + 2u - 1 <= -eps): they are correct with the stated definitions of a, s, l, z, u, v. Issues: the strict-optimality margin eps is an arbitrary tuning constant that changes the feasible set; no sensitivity to eps is shown; the big-M constants M, Omega, delta are unspecified; and "exact" is a property of the formulation, whereas the experiments show the solver stalling at T=80, K=2 (Table 3: full MILP 0.205 vs 0.089 at true parameters). Do not call the MILP an "exact estimator" in the abstract without qualification.

## 3. Algorithmic contribution

- **Exact one-goal algorithm.** Correct (see Theorem 2) and fast on the data used. The computational study (Table 4) is on random AR(1) instances with 10 instances per size; fine. The claim "the heuristic ... at 10-40 times the run time" (p. 12) is contradicted by Table 4: at T=25 it is 4.1 s vs 0.02 s (about 200 times); at T=50 about 41 times; at T=100 about 10 times. The text should say 10-200 times.
- **Multi-goal heuristic: inadequately evaluated.**
  - The only evidence is Table 3, one design (K=2, T=80), apparently one run per row, no replications, no parameter-recovery errors (the text says "approximately, weights" but shows nothing), and no comparison to a proven optimum. The heuristic does not reach the true-parameter loss at noise 0.1 (0.111 vs 0.106) and needs 267-925 s.
  - The one place where the heuristic *can* be checked against the exact optimum (K=1 aid, Section 5.1) shows it is up to 5% above the optimum for 5 of 10 donors and "changed conclusions for two donors". Yet all K=2 central-bank results (Tables 7, 8, 9, appendix) rest on this heuristic, "one search seed per fit" in the robustness appendix, and the model comparison states the bank models are heuristic fits. There is no reason to believe the 14 bank estimates are global minimisers.
  - Requested: (i) a replication study for K=2 with parameter recovery, at the sizes of the real data (T = 30-100, 7-8 free parameters); (ii) for K=2 a certified lower bound (MILP dual bound, or a grid-based exact solver over (alpha_1, alpha_2) with the K=1 machinery per goal when goals are weakly coupled), or at least multi-seed stability of the 14 central-bank estimates; (iii) report how many random starts, how often the same optimum was found.
- No certificate for K>=2 exists; the "Extensions" section admits this. The abstract's "scalable heuristic" is fair; "estimator" for K>=2 is not exact.

## 4. Empirical work

### 4.1 Central banks (Sections 6-7, Tables 7-9, appendix)

I recomputed the absolute errors from tab_cb_exploratory.tex and tab_cb_confirmatory.tex.

1. **The "implied target beats average inflation" result is driven by one observation (Turkey) and by conditioning on the benchmark's failure.**
   - Confirmatory (5 banks): implied errors IL 1.49, NZ 0.34, CO 0.02, HU 1.97, TR 1.14 (MAE 0.99); mean-inflation errors 0.23, 0.54, 0.79, 0.33, 5.70 (MAE 1.52). Dropping Turkey: implied MAE 0.96 vs mean-inflation 0.47. Pooled over all 14 banks: implied 0.64 vs mean 0.85; without Turkey 0.61 vs 0.47. So the implied target is *worse* than the benchmark in 13 of 14 banks on average; the aggregate advantage is one country.
   - The confirmatory "success criterion" (docs/09) is evaluated on only banks where average inflation deviated by more than 0.5 pp from target. That selection conditions on the benchmark being bad. Any estimator that is not mean inflation will tend to win on this subset (regression to the mean). With n=3 the sign test cannot be significant (p=1/8 under a coin flip); with "7 of 7" the exploratory four are not independent evidence because the specification was chosen after seeing them (Section 6, Development).
   - NZ passes the 0.5 pp filter by only 0.04 pp (mean 2.54 vs target 2.0).
2. **A trivial prior benchmark is missing and would be competitive.** Most inflation targets lie between 2 and 3 percent. A constant "2.5" has absolute errors (recomputed from the tables): exploratory 0.5, 0.5, 0, 0.5, 0, 0.5, 0.5, 1.5, 0 (MAE 0.44, the same as the implied-target MAE of 0.45) and confirmatory 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 2.5 (MAE 0.90 vs 0.99). The relevant comparison for an "implied target" estimator is (i) such a prior or shrinkage benchmark, (ii) the standard Taylor-rule / Dennis-type implied-target estimators that the paper cites in the introduction, and (iii) a longer-sample or median inflation. None is reported.
3. **A natural validation is missing: the band.** Israel and New Zealand (target range 1-3%) and several others have official tolerance bands; the model estimates b. Comparing b_hat to the official band half-width is a second ground-truth test that is available at no cost.
4. **Robustness is weak and honestly reported but under-weighted in the abstract.** In Appendix Table 11, 2- and 6-quarter horizons produce 3 and 2 degenerate fits out of 14, the implied-MAE is 0.80 and 0.93 vs 1.02 and 0.42 for the benchmark, and ending the sample in 2015 gives 0.75 vs 0.48. I verified the 0.80 and 0.94 means from the table. So the headline is robust in 1 of 4 specifications. The abstract mentions "the advantage is sensitive" but still leads with "all seven cases".
5. **Pre-registration.** docs/09 states the document was "written and committed on 2026-11-22" and the results were run on 2026-11-22, i.e. the same day, in the author's own repository (history can be rewritten). The registered hypothesis was already known to hold in the nine exploratory banks. Candidate list contains Indonesia (ID), which appears neither among the included nor the excluded banks in the paper or in docs/09's result paragraph. The paper does not report the three excluded banks (CZ, KR, IS) or ID. The pre-registered primary outcome (MAE over all included banks, outcome 1) is reported but its Turkey dependence is not stressed in the paper text; docs/09 itself says "The overall MAE advantage depends heavily on Turkey". The paper should say the same. For credibility use an external timestamp (OSF/AsPredicted) in the future, and describe this one as "pre-specified" rather than "pre-registered".
6. **Bootstrap uncertainty (Table 9) is not informative.**
   - The intervals use B=20 replicates, so the "5%" and "95%" columns are essentially the minimum and maximum of 20 draws, not quantiles. Intervals such as IN (-292.7, 5.9), TR (-575.5, 9.0), IL (0.65, 16.9), PL (-13.4, 5.2) are vacuous, and "covers stated: yes" for these has no content. The statement "Eleven of fourteen intervals contain the stated target" is therefore close to uninformative.
   - The bootstrap median often differs sharply from the point estimate (IL 3.49 vs 2.20; NZ 1.66 vs 2.84; HU 4.97 vs 4.09), which indicates bias / instability of the estimator, and the intervals do not contain the point estimate in some cases. A percentile bootstrap is not justified for a non-regular estimator with parameters at the boundary.
   - Report tau only when alpha is bounded away from its bound; report intervals for (c, alpha) or for the band edges e+/-, which stay finite.
   - Mexico's narrow interval (3.24-3.89) excludes the stated 3%, so narrow intervals do not mean accurate; the claim that "the width separates reliable from unreliable estimates" needs a test of coverage across banks.
7. **Specification issues.** (a) The state uses *realised* inflation four quarters ahead, which already includes the effect of the decision being modelled (the model needs the counterfactual "unchanged-policy" value x_t). (b) The policy-rate *level* is not in the state, although for a Taylor-type rule the stopping point of rate changes depends on the neutral rate, so "inflation at which rates stop moving" is not the target unless rates are at neutral. (c) Decisions are discrete 25 bp moves with many zeros, which a Laplace-noise decision-space loss does not describe. (d) Windows are short (n = 32-104 quarters) relative to 8 free parameters (K=2) and several fits hit the alpha bound. (e) Dropping quarters with policy rate <= 0.25% selects the sample on the outcome variable of interest.
8. **Specification search.** Appendix A lists six specifications; the "reported" one is the last, chosen after the earlier ones failed (MAE 0.72-1.83 vs 0.47). The Development paragraph acknowledges this. The correct statement is that the confirmatory evidence is the five-bank test, which is where the Turkey issue arises.

### 4.2 Aid (Section 8, Tables 5-6, Fig. 5)

1. **The permutation test does not test "goal-seeking".** The null is that decisions are exchangeable and unrelated to levels. Any serial dependence in decisions, regime shifts, trends, momentum, or mean reversion of any kind rejects it. The paper's own placebo shows partial adjustment and the QP rule are rejected in 100% of series (Table 6). So a rejection means "decisions depend on the level in some way". Calling this "goal-seeking" and then interpreting a band is a leap. The size check (2% at 5%, 7% at 10% in 200 simulated series, which I confirmed from permutation_test.csv; Monte Carlo SE about 1.8 pp at 7%) covers only i.i.d. state-independent decisions. Real ODA changes are persistent and have trends (the 1970-1975 rise, 1990s fall, 2005 rise, 2018 definitional break). A size check with persistent, trending, non-goal-seeking decision processes is required.
2. **Multiple testing.** 23 donors are tested. With 199 permutations the minimum attainable p is 0.005, so Bonferroni at 10% (0.0043) rejects none. Benjamini-Hochberg at 10% (my recomputation from the sorted p-values: 0.005, 0.010, 0.010, 0.010, 0.015, 0.040, 0.040, 0.040, 0.055, 0.060, ...) gives only the first five donors. The abstract's "14 of 23 at 10%" and "8 at 5%" are unadjusted. Report adjusted values or a global test, and use more permutations (B >= 999).
3. **Comparison with national goals is selective and post hoc.**
   - Five donors with "verified" goals were examined after the estimates existed. Many other donors had a 0.7% national or EU timetable (Belgium, Finland, France, Germany, Austria, Ireland, Spain, Italy, UK) whose revealed targets (BEL 0.48, FIN 0.59, FRA 0.56, DEU 0.57) lie well *below* 0.7; those would be counterexamples or ambiguous, and they are not discussed. The paper must pre-specify the set of donors with a documented goal and report all.
   - For Sweden the goal 1.0 lies outside the reported near-optimal set [0.88, 0.95] and the band [0.83, 0.97]; "close to" has no definition or test. Provide a test of H0: tau = goal (e.g. profile-likelihood-type), or at least report whether the goal lies in the set.
   - Inconsistent comparison parameter. For aid the *midpoint* tau is compared with national goals; for defence the *lower edge* (1.99) is compared with the 2% guideline. A 0.7% commitment is a floor in the same sense as NATO's 2%, so by the paper's own defence logic the lower edge is the relevant parameter (Sweden 0.83, Norway 0.93, Denmark 0.71 vs goal 0.8). Fix one rule ex ante and apply it to both.
   - Goals changed over the 1960-2024 sample (UN 0.7 in 1970, EU 2005, Sweden dropping 1% in 2023, Netherlands 0.8 to 0.7), while the fit assumes constant parameters. A rolling or subperiod fit is needed.
4. **Data consistency.** Section 7 (Data) says grant-equivalent ODA from 2018 onwards; Section 8 says from 2019. The level path crosses a definitional break (net disbursement to grant equivalent) that shifts the level for donors with large loan components, so a time-invariant band is misspecified across the break even though the single change is dropped; the permutation scheme "keeping the jumps at the 2018 break" is not clearly defined. The state x_t is the previous year's share, ignoring that an unchanged ODA volume changes the share through GNI growth (a mechanical drift in x). Use ODA_{t-1}/GNI_t as the counterfactual.
5. **Near-optimal sets are ad hoc.** The "2% tolerance" set has no coverage calibration. The placebo shows 10% of pure-noise series produce a set narrower than one unit. Some sets are internally odd: Turkey's band is a point [0.56, 0.56] while its set is [-0.27, 0.58]; Luxembourg's band [1.00, 1.00] with p=0.06.
6. **Text-table inconsistencies.** Text says "United States, 0.22"; Table 5 gives 0.21. Discussion says revealed targets of "about 0.15-0.3%", which includes Italy, Korea and Spain, none of which passes the goal-seeking test (abstract: "about 0.2% to about 1%" among goal-seekers). The abstract writes "the revealed targets are close to those goals (p<=0.01)", but those p-values test goal-seeking, not closeness to the goal. Step sizes in Table 5 are implausible in places (Sweden step down 0.43 pp of GNI per year, Portugal 0.35) and are not discussed; they look like single-episode artefacts.
7. **Model comparison (Table 10).** The paper's own placebo logic says model comparison identifies misspecification when partial adjustment wins. On real data aggregate PA error is below GP error (aid 0.052 vs 0.056; banks 0.413 vs 0.481), and holding beats both. The paper acknowledges non-predictive value but then continues to interpret the GP bands as aspiration levels. By the author's own placebo (partial-adjustment series also yield tau = 1.99-2.00), the target recovered is not evidence for the *goal-program* structure; the PA estimator recovers the target equally well in the placebo. The comparison of revealed target with the PA implied target in five donors ("closer in three cases") is n=5 and relies on the PA implied target being undefined for 7 donors.

### 4.3 Defence

- The headline "the revealed lower edge was 1.99% ... matching the 2% guideline" is a point estimate with no uncertainty, no permutation test (the test was run for aid only: permutation_test.csv has only aid and calibration rows), and no profile set. Provide all three.
- Table 8: in 2015-2021 the mean level is 1.48 and the band is [1.99, 3.48], so the great majority of ally-years lie below the band and are predicted to rise by exactly the step 0.03. The fit is therefore "constant drift of 0.03 for most allies" plus a few ally-years that stopped; the lower edge is determined by a handful of high spenders (US, Greece, UK, Estonia, Poland, etc.). Leave-one-ally-out and cluster-by-ally resampling are needed (observations are not independent across allies in a common year; NATO decisions are strongly correlated).
- Pooling 31 allies with common parameters is strongly restrictive (the US is at >3% and falling in 2015-2017, Iceland has no military). The 2025 value is an estimate. The period boundaries (2015-2021, 2022-2025) were chosen by the author around known events; no change-point test. The statement that "the shift of the lower edge is consistent with ... 3.5%" is therefore not evidence.
- Sensitivity to the data source is not reported: results/inverse_interval_gp_budgets.csv (earlier heuristic run) gives different values for the same NATO series (tau_hat 1.42 for 2015-2021, 2.2 for 2022-2025) and COFOG-based defence shares give 1.2 and 1.5. These are not reproduced or reconciled in the paper; at minimum explain which quantity (tau vs lower edge) they correspond to, and why the earlier heuristic fits differ so much from the exact ones (consistent with the heuristic being unreliable).
- The comparison with the 2% guideline is also conceptually loose: the 2014 Wales pledge was to move toward 2% within a decade, not a floor at which allies stop.

### 4.4 Firms

Mostly a negative result, honestly stated, and I commend that. But (i) up to 3,000 firm-years per industry are used with no description of how they were sampled, (ii) leverage changes mix decisions with retained earnings and valuation changes, so "decision" is not well defined, (iii) the depository-institution paragraph over-interprets a band edge at the boundary of the parameter range (upper edge 1.00). The abstract's "not informative for corporate leverage" is the right message; the SIC 60 paragraph should be shortened or removed. Table 8 lists SIC 67 with band [0.61, 1.23], an upper edge above 1.

### 4.5 Placebo, permutation size, model comparison

- The placebo section is a good idea and the numbers match the CSV (coin-flip: gain 3%, set width 3.54; 10% of series with set < 1; GP beats hold in 17%; I confirmed these from misspec_placebo.csv). But the placebo draws one noise level (Laplace 0.03) and one parameter configuration, with an AR(1) state *plus* decisions "added to the state", which is a different state-law from the real data. Vary the noise level, T and the persistence.
- The in-sample "gain over holding" is described as "non-negative by construction", which is correct, and it is exactly why it is a weak diagnostic; the paper then relies on a permutation p-value, whose limitation is in 4.2.1.
- The "model comparison" for central banks uses test windows of 10-32 quarters and no significance tests (acknowledged). Report paired tests (e.g. Diebold-Mariano with HAC) or drop the strong statements.

## 7. Recommendation and justification

**Major revision.** The one-goal theory (breakpoint structure, closed-form clip policy, exact enumeration algorithm with a grid certificate) is correct (modulo the fixes above), and the honest reporting of negative results (no out-of-sample predictive advantage, horizon sensitivity, non-informative firm results, size problems of naive diagnostics) is commendable and unusual. However, the paper's headline empirical claims are not supported at the level at which they are stated: the central-bank advantage rests on one country and on a benchmark that is weak by construction; the aid "goal-seeking" test cannot separate goal programs from generic state-dependence and is not multiplicity-adjusted; the comparison with national goals is post hoc with a changing comparison parameter; the defence result has no uncertainty quantification; and the multiple-objective part (K>=2, the reason this paper fits a special issue on multiple-objective and goal programming) has no identification theory and an unverified heuristic. With the benchmarks, robustness and uncertainty analyses above, and a toned-down abstract, this could become a solid contribution on exact inverse estimation of single-goal interval programs with a careful empirical illustration. In its current form, the abstract and Discussion over-claim relative to the evidence, and the statements about priorities, identification and multi-goal estimation are not yet established.
